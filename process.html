01 · Ideation

How I chose the topic

I spent the summer and the start of the fall looking for a topic. I wanted a real machine learning question that I could run on my own computer, and I went through a lot of ideas before one fit.

What I was looking for

  • A real machine learning problem about how AI itself behaves, not an AI app for some industry
  • One clear failure to study, with one main thing to vary and real baselines to compare against
  • Something I could explain in one sentence to someone who doesn't work with AI
  • Enough published research around it to build on, but a specific question nobody has answered yet
  • Doable with public data and open models on my own computer

Ideas I dropped

  • Benchmarking Python dataframe libraries
  • Behavior drift in deployed AI models
  • AI tutoring applications An AI app for one field, not a question about how AI itself works
  • How quantization affects a model's memory of rare facts
  • AI visual assistants Turned into several ideas stitched together instead of one question
  • Preserving dissenting views in AI meeting summaries
  • Safeguards for AI-generated code optimizations
  • Committees of AI judges
  • Very narrow "gap in one benchmark" topics Too narrow to matter outside one benchmark
  • A final shortlist: why probes trained on one model fail on another, noise from how benchmarks are served, and comparing reasoning methods at equal compute Each one was too hard to explain in one sentence to someone outside the field

Where I landed

I settled on measuring when a mistake in a conversation becomes permanent. The paper that got me there was Laban et al.'s LLMs Get Lost in Multi-Turn Conversation, which found that once a model takes a wrong turn, it gets lost and doesn't recover. That made me wonder whether recovery stops being possible at a specific point, and whether that point can be measured. I call the stretch between the first error and that point the recovery window.

02 · Background literature

What's already known

Before designing anything, I collected and summarized 40 sources across five categories, then wrote a literature review around the 25 most relevant ones. The research falls into three groups.

  1. 1

    Conversations make models worse

    Laban et al. found a 39% average drop across more than 200,000 simulated conversations. Most of that drop came from models becoming less consistent, while their best-case ability barely changed. Other teams found similar drops on different kinds of tasks, and studies using survival analysis model the turn where a model first fails.

  2. 2

    There are three competing explanations

    A model's own earlier answers may mislead it, instructions given in pieces may get misread, and user pushback may make it cave. The studies disagree with each other, and each one used different tasks and different measurements.

  3. 3

    Models rarely fix themselves

    Even when a model seems to register a mistake internally, it usually doesn't correct it. Giving it outside help only brings part of the accuracy back.

Existing work measures whether a model is wrong at a given moment. It hasn't measured how long after a mistake the conversation can still be repaired, or which of the three explanations shortens that time.

      04 · Methodology

      The research plan

      In plain terms, the question is this: once a model makes a mistake in a conversation, how many turns do you have to fix it? My hypothesis is that the longer a mistake stays in a conversation, the less likely it is that giving the model the right information will fix it.

      1. 1

        Tasks

        I'm using math and coding problems because a program can check the answers, either with unit tests or an exact numeric match. No AI model grades another model's work.

      2. 2

        Screening

        I only keep problems that every model solves at least 90% of the time when the whole problem is asked at once. That way a wrong answer later in a conversation can't just mean the model was never able to solve it.

      3. 3

        Models

        The models are small open-weight models that run locally on my own computer. Running them myself means I control every setting.

      4. 4

        Three things I vary

        Each problem's instructions are split across several turns. Between conditions I change whether the model can see its own earlier replies, how the instructions are broken up, and whether a simulated user pushes back.

      5. 5

        The repair probe

        At each turn after the model's first error, I copy the conversation, give the copy the correct missing information, and ask again. If even the repaired copy gets it wrong, the conversation has passed the point of no return. The real conversation keeps going untouched.

      6. 6

        What I measure

        The recovery window is the number of turns between the first error and the first failed repair. Each probe will be sampled three times, and I'll report results at horizons of 3, 5 and 8 turns, since "irreversible" can only ever mean the conversation didn't recover within that many turns.

      Where things stand · November 2026 The plan is set, and I'm building the code that runs the test conversations now. I haven't run any tests yet, so there are no results on this site.
