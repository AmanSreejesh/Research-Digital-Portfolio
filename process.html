01 Ideation

Choosing the topic

I spent the summer and the start of the fall looking for a topic. I wanted a real machine learning question that I could run on my own computer, and I went through a lot of ideas before one fit.

What I was looking for

  • A real machine learning problem about how AI itself behaves, not an AI app for some industry
  • One clear failure to study, with one main thing to vary and real baselines to compare against
  • Something I could explain in one sentence to someone who doesn't work with AI
  • Enough published research to build on, but a specific question nobody has answered yet
  • Doable with public data and open models on my own computer

Ideas I dropped

  • Benchmarking Python dataframe libraries
  • Behavior drift in deployed AI models
  • AI tutoring applications An AI app for one field, not a question about how AI itself works
  • How quantization affects a model's memory of rare facts
  • AI visual assistants Turned into several ideas stitched together instead of one question
  • Preserving dissenting views in AI meeting summaries
  • Safeguards for AI-generated code optimizations
  • Committees of AI judges
  • Very narrow "gap in one benchmark" topics Too narrow to matter outside one benchmark
  • A final shortlist about probes, benchmark noise and reasoning methods Each one was too hard to explain in one sentence to someone outside the field

Where I landed

The recovery window

Laban et al.'s paper LLMs Get Lost in Multi-Turn Conversation found that once a model takes a wrong turn in a conversation, it gets lost and doesn't recover. That made me wonder whether recovery stops being possible at a specific point, and whether that point can be measured.

02 Background literature

What's already known

Before designing anything, I collected and summarized 40 sources, then wrote a literature review around the most relevant ones.

40sources collected and summarized
5categories, from foundational papers to popular media
25sources in the literature review

The research falls into three groups.

  1. 1

    Conversations make models worse

    Laban et al. found a 39% average drop across more than 200,000 simulated conversations. Most of that drop came from models becoming less consistent, while their best-case ability barely changed. Other teams found similar drops on different kinds of tasks.

  2. 2

    Nobody agrees on why

    A model's own earlier answers may mislead it, instructions given in pieces may get misread, or user pushback may make it cave. Each study tested its explanation on different tasks with different measurements, so the results are hard to compare.

  3. 3

    Models rarely fix themselves

    Even when a model seems to register a mistake internally, it usually doesn't correct it. Outside help only brings part of the accuracy back.

The gap

Existing work measures whether a model is wrong at a given moment. It hasn't measured how long after a mistake the conversation can still be repaired.

      03 Methodology

      The research plan

      Once a model makes a mistake in a conversation, how many turns do you have to fix it? Here's how I'm setting up the experiment to find out.

      Hypothesis

      The longer a mistake stays in a conversation, the less likely it is that giving the model the right information will fix it.

      1. 01

        Tasks

        Math and coding problems, because a program can check the answers with unit tests or an exact number. No AI grades another AI.

      2. 02

        Screening

        I only keep problems every model solves at least 90% of the time when asked all at once, so a later mistake can't mean the model just couldn't do it.

      3. 03

        Models

        Small open-weight models running on my own computer, so I control every setting.

      4. 04

        Three things I vary

        Whether the model can see its own earlier replies, how the instructions are broken up, and whether a simulated user pushes back.

      5. 05

        The repair probe

        After the first mistake, I copy the conversation at each turn, give the copy the missing information, and ask again. The real conversation keeps going untouched.

      6. 06

        What I measure

        How many turns pass between the first mistake and the first repair that no longer works.

      Where things stand · November 2026

      The plan is set, and I'm building the code that runs the test conversations now. No tests have run yet, so there are no results on this site.
